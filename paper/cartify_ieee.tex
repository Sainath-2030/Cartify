\begin{abstract}
\noindent
Retail recommendation is frequently deployed as an isolated model layered on top of
an operational database, which leaves two problems unsolved. First, catalogues
assembled from third-party marketplaces carry enough structural noise that the
signals consumed downstream are systematically biased; a recommender trained on
unfiltered inventory learns from mislabelled and duplicated items. Second,
single-modality recommenders are brittle: collaborative signals vanish for
unseen users, visual signals generalise poorly to intent, and sequence signals
are unavailable outside an active session. This paper describes \emph{Cartify}, a
working dual-layer commerce platform that treats recommendation as one consumer
of an auditable analytical pipeline rather than as an isolated model. The
operational tier is a normalised transactional store that emits a typed event
telemetry stream; an extract-transform-load stage subjects the catalogue to a
ten-rule quality gate that certifies 17{,}931 of 572{,}083 scanned records and
records every rejection with a reason, then loads survivors into a star schema
alongside provenance and quality-audit relations. The mining tier trains four
representation learners --- a generalised-matrix-factorisation and perceptron
collaborative network, a frozen ResNet-18 visual encoder, a gated recurrent
session model, and a collaborative denoising autoencoder --- and fuses them
through a context-conditioned attention layer whose weights are learned per
request. Classical miners run alongside: Apriori over order baskets,
recency-frequency-monetary segmentation, and an interpretable churn scorer.
Evaluation under leave-one-out protocol with 99 sampled negatives shows the
fusion layer recovering 2.2 times the catalogue coverage of its strongest
neural component while retaining competitive ranking quality, and the learned
attention distribution assigns 55.6\% weight to collaborative evidence and
12.4\% to appearance, quantifying how little visual information a
behaviour-driven catalogue actually carries.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Commercial recommender systems are usually presented as a modelling problem in
isolation: given an interaction matrix, produce a ranked list. Production systems
are rarely so tidy. The catalogue that feeds such a model is assembled from
heterogeneous third-party feeds, each with its own identifier conventions,
duplicate policies, and category taxonomies. Interaction logs accumulate unevenly
across event types, so a ``view'' and a ``purchase'' arrive with very different
frequencies and very different meanings. Where behavioural signal is thin,
content-derived signal is needed; where appearance is ambiguous, behavioural
signal is needed. Handling both regimes with one model requires a mechanism to
 arbitrate between them per user and per item rather than per dataset.

\emph{Cartify} is a dual-layer commerce platform built around that arbitration
problem. It separates an operational tier, normalised to third normal form for
transactional correctness, from an analytical tier modelled as a star schema for
aggregate querying. Between them sits an extract-transform-load (ETL) stage whose
explicit purpose is to make the catalogue trustworthy before any model consumes
it. Above the warehouse, four representation learners produce embeddings of a
user--item pair from four distinct evidence types, and a lightweight attention
network learns, at inference time, how much to trust each one.

The contributions of this work are as follows.

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{A quality-gated catalogue pipeline.} We contribute an ETL stage
that applies ten deterministic rejection rules to raw marketplace records and
emits an auditable ledger of every rejection, retaining 17{,}931 certified
products from 572{,}083 scanned records while discarding 4{,}653 records with
machine-readable reasons. Section~\ref{sec:etl} reports the rule taxonomy and
its distribution.

\item \textbf{An event-typed telemetry contract.} We contribute a typed
interaction schema in which every behavioural event carries a discriminated
category and an optional product reference, together with a session identifier
and a monotonic timestamp. This single contract serves three consumers with
different needs: collaborative filtering consumes weighted user--item pairs,
sequence modelling consumes session-ordered token streams, and the analytical
tier consumes the same rows as additive measures. Section~\ref{sec:arch}
describes the contract and its dual consumption.

\item \textbf{Context-conditioned attention fusion.} We contribute a fusion
layer that forms its query from a subset of modalities and its keys from all
modalities, adds a learned per-modality salience term, and produces a
softmax distribution over four evidence sources that varies per
(user, item) pair rather than per dataset. Section~\ref{sec:fusion} derives the
formulation and Section~\ref{sec:eval} reports the learned distribution.

\item \textbf{Classical mining in the same loop.} We contribute an Apriori
basket miner, an RFM segmentation stage, and an interpretable churn scorer that
combines a calibrated linear score with an eight-rule decision layer, all
exposed to operators through a business-intelligence console. These stages are
deliberately transparent, and Section~\ref{sec:classical} is explicit that the
rule confidences are design parameters rather than measured frequencies.

\item \textbf{A reproducible evaluation harness.} We contribute a leave-one-out
protocol with 99 sampled unseen negatives, reporting hit ratio, normalised
discounted cumulative gain, precision, recall, mean reciprocal rank, catalogue
coverage, and diversity for all five models under one protocol, together with
the raw metrics artifact. Section~\ref{sec:eval} reports results and
Section~\ref{sec:discussion} discusses their limitations, including one result
that contradicts the system's own design hypothesis.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Background and Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\subsection{Collaborative Filtering}

Matrix factorisation established the practical baseline for collaborative
filtering, decomposing a sparse user--item matrix into low-rank latent factors
trained by alternating least squares or by gradient descent on an implicit
objective~\cite{mnih2008pmf,hu2008icdm}. Rendle et al.~\cite{rendle2009bpr}
popularised pairwise Bayesian Personalised Ranking, which optimises a
preference ordering rather than a pointwise likelihood and remains a strong
baseline in explicit-feedback settings.

Implicit feedback changes the problem substantially. A missing interaction is
weak evidence of dislike, so pointwise binary cross-entropy is mis-specified and
requires negative sampling to remain tractable. He et al.~\cite{he2017ncf}
addressed this with NeuMF, which fuses a generalised matrix factorisation
branch with a multilayer perceptron branch and trains both on sampled negatives.
We adopt this architecture as the collaborative component of Cartify because the
two branches capture complementary structure: the GMF branch represents latent
similarity linearly, while the perceptron branch represents non-linear
interaction effects. Our fusion layer preserves that property at the modality
level.

Denoising autoencoders provide a complementary formulation. Rather than
factoring user and item independently, a collaborative autoencoder reconstructs a
user's interaction vector from a corrupted input and constrains the
reconstruction through the user's own latent vector, which both denoises and
regularises~\cite{ma2015autorec}. Because the decoder can be made
bias-free, predictions derive strictly from user preference rather than from a
dataset-wide popularity offset. We adopt this construction as a fourth evidence
source for reasons given in Section~\ref{sec:ae}.

\subsection{Sequential Recommendation}

Session-based recommendation models the next item as a function of a
chronologically ordered prefix. Gated recurrent units suit this setting because
the gating mechanism lets a sequence-modeling network retain or overwrite
partial session state, which mitigates the vanishing-gradient behaviour of
plain recurrent networks~\cite{ho2019grugraphs,chung2015gru4rec}. Chung et
al.~\cite{chung2015gru4rec} applied GRUs with element-wise and dot-product
scoring to session data; subsequent work addressed the tension between parallel
training and sequential dependencies.

GRU models are strongest exactly where collaborative models are weakest --- inside
an active session --- and weakest at cold start, where the embedding table has
never seen the user. This complementary failure profile motivates treating them
as separate modalities rather than as substitutes.

\subsection{Multi-Source and Attention-Based Fusion}

Combining heterogeneous recommendation signals has a long history. The naive
combination, averaging or summing scores, assumes the sources are commensurable.
Attention-based fusion replaces fixed weights with input-conditioned weights.
Zhou et al.~\cite{zhou2018din} showed that a local activation unit can isolate
the subset of a user's historical behaviour relevant to a candidate item, and
this locality principle is what makes per-pair weighting meaningful.
Zhang et al.~\cite{zhang2019nais} extended multiplicative interaction to
candidate-aware selection.

Our fusion layer differs in one respect we consider important: the attention
\emph{query} is formed from a strict subset of modalities (collaborative,
sequential, and denoised-latent) while the \emph{keys} span all four. This
asymmetry makes the query a summary of user and session state and the keys a
summary of each evidence type, so a modality's weight reflects its agreement
with user state rather than its own magnitude.

\subsection{Classical Data Mining}

Market basket analysis discovers co-occurrence structure in transaction
databases. Apriori~\cite{agrawal1994survey} performs breadth-first candidate
generation with the anti-monotonicity pruning property, and FP-Growth~\cite{han2000fpgrowth}
replaces candidate generation with a compact tree representation. Customer
segmentation typically uses the recency--frequency--monetary
framework~\cite{fildes2002retail}, with clustering applied to the scaled RFM
vector. Churn modelling combines probabilistic scoring with rule-based
overlays so that a risk tier carries a reason a human can audit.

Cartify runs these classical miners deliberately alongside the neural stack (reproduction procedure in Appendix~\ref{app:mining}). They
are not superseded by it. Basket rules are directly actionable for merchandising,
RFM segments are interpretable without a data scientist in the loop, and a churn
tier with a named rule is auditable in a way a probability is not.

\subsection{Data Warehousing and OLAP}

The warehouse pattern separates a normalised operational store designed for
transactional correctness from an analytical store designed for aggregation
~\cite{inmon2002building,kimball2013toolkit}. Star schemas denormalise
dimensions around one or more fact tables~\cite{simon2005dw}, and OLAP operations
such as slice, dice, roll-up, and drill-down are defined over that
structure~\cite{chaudhuri1997olap,silberschatz2020database}. Data quality
dimensions are standard practice~\cite{han2012datamining}; we additionally
persist provenance so that any reported metric can be traced to the extraction
that produced it.

Data preprocessing surveys~\cite{han2012datamining} catalogue techniques for
missing values, noise, and outliers. Our contribution here is narrow and
practical: we show that for marketplace catalogues, a modest set of
deterministic rules applied at ingestion time removes more harmful training
signal than any post-hoc filter applied after collection.

\section{Motivation and Problem Statement}
\label{sec:motivation}

\subsection{The Catalogue Is the Model's Real Input}

A recommender is only as sound as the catalogue it indexes. Consider a catalogue
assembled from 160 category feeds. Within it, a product may appear under several
identifiers, a single product may be listed twice with different titles, a
category may contain items belonging to an adjacent category, and a price may be
recorded as zero or absent. Each defect is individually small. In aggregate they
bias the item embedding table: duplicated items split statistical mass across
two indices, miscategorised items corrupt the content prior, and phantom low-cost
items pollute any price-conditioned feature.

The failure is difficult to detect downstream because it is invisible in
aggregate accuracy. A recommender trained on a noisy catalogue produces a model
that scores well on the noisy catalogue. Quality assurance must therefore
precede modelling, not follow it. This motivates treating ingestion-time
validation as a first-class pipeline stage with its own audit relation
(Section~\ref{sec:etl}).

\subsection{Four Evidence Types, Four Failure Modes}

Let a user be $u$ and an item be $i$. Four sources of evidence about the
affinity $y(u,i)$ are available, and each fails in a characteristic regime:

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Collaborative} $f_{\mathrm{ncf}}(u,i)$: observed user--item
co-occurrence. Fails when $u$ or $i$ is unseen.
\item \textbf{Visual} $f_{\mathrm{cnn}}(i)$: appearance of the item image.
Independent of $u$, so it survives a cold user, but it cannot express intent and
is easily confused across visually similar but functionally distinct items.
\item \textbf{Sequential} $f_{\mathrm{gru}}(u,i)$: position of $i$ in an
active session. Sharp while a session is live, undefined outside one.
\item \textbf{Denoised latent} $f_{\mathrm{ae}}(u,i)$: reconstruction of the
user's interaction vector. Generalises to sparse users, and degrades toward a
popularity baseline for very sparse ones.
\end{enumerate}

A single model cannot cover all four regimes. The operational question is
therefore not which model to choose but how to weight the four adaptively.

\subsection{Formal Objective}

Given a user--item pair $(u,i)$, let
$\mathbf{f} = [f_{\mathrm{ncf}}; f_{\mathrm{cnn}}; f_{\mathrm{gru}}; f_{\mathrm{ae}}]$
be the concatenated evidence vector and let $\mathbf{1}$ be the modality mask.
We seek a predictor $s_\theta(u,i) \in (0,1)$ and a weighting
$\boldsymbol{\alpha}(u,i)$ over modalities such that

\begin{equation}
\boldsymbol{\alpha}(u,i) = \operatorname{softmax}\!\left(
  \frac{q(\mathbf{f})^{\top} k(\mathbf{f})}{\sqrt{d_a}} + s(\mathbf{f})
\right), \qquad
\sum_{m=1}^{4} \alpha_m = 1,
\label{eq:alpha}
\end{equation}

\begin{equation}
s_\theta(u,i) = \sigma\!\left( h\!\left( \sum_{m=1}^{4} \alpha_m(u,i)\, \phi_m(\mathbf{f}) \right) \right),
\label{eq:score}
\end{equation}

where $q(\cdot)$ forms the query from the state-bearing modalities only, $k(\cdot)$
forms keys from all four, $s(\cdot)$ is a per-modality salience term, $\phi_m$ is
a modality-specific projection to a shared dimension, $\sigma$ is the logistic
function, and $h$ is a prediction head. Equations~\eqref{eq:alpha} and
\eqref{eq:score} are implemented in Section~\ref{sec:fusion}. The requirement
that $\boldsymbol{\alpha}$ be a function of the pair, not of the dataset, is what
distinguishes this from fixed score averaging.

\subsection{Operational Tier}

The operational schema is normalised to third normal form. Orders, order items,
products, categories, users, carts, cart items, wishlists, reviews, and
interactions are separate relations with foreign keys and check constraints.
This normalisation is deliberate and is not a stylistic choice: checkout must
enforce referential integrity and partial rollback under concurrency, and
denormalisation for read performance would compromise that. Read acceleration is
handled instead by composite indexes on the filter paths actually used
(category, brand, price, rating, availability) and by full-text search over
product titles.

The backend is layered strictly as routes, middleware, controllers, services,
models. Routes bind URLs and middleware; controllers own request and response
handling; services own business logic; models are the only layer that issues SQL,
and they do so exclusively through parameterised statements. This constraint is
what allows the mining tier to be reasoned about independently, since all
analytical queries originate in a small, auditable set of model functions.

\subsection{The Telemetry Contract}
\label{sec:telemetry}

The `interactions' relation is the system's most consequential design decision,
because four downstream consumers with incompatible needs all read it. Its
schema is:

\begin{equation}
\mathcal{I} = \langle u, i, \tau, \sigma, \chi, \mu, \text{created\_at} \rangle
\label{eq:interaction}
\end{equation}

where $u$ is the user (nullable for anonymous traffic), $i$ the product
(nullable for events not tied to an item), $\tau$ the interaction type drawn from
an enumerated vocabulary, $\sigma$ the session identifier, $\chi$ the client
that emitted the event, and $\mu$ a JSONB metadata bag.

Three properties of this tuple, Eq.~\eqref{eq:interaction}, deserve emphasis.

\paragraph{Nullable product reference.} Events such as a search or a category
browse are real behavioural evidence but carry no product identifier. Rather
than inventing a placeholder item, the relation permits a null reference, and
pairwise learners skip null rows explicitly. This is a small design decision
that avoids a silent correctness bug: had nulls been coerced to a sentinel item,
the collaborative embedding table would have acquired a spuriously dominant
index.

\paragraph{Session continuity.} The session identifier, combined with timestamp
ordering, is what allows a sequence model to reconstruct a browsing trajectory
from an append-only event table with no additional client-side state.

\paragraph{Extensible metadata.} Because $\mu$ is JSONB, new event types and
model-specific fields are added without schema migration. This mattered in
practice: the sequence and fusion tiers store derived session state in $\mu$
rather than in new columns.

Table~\ref{tab:telemetry} lists the event vocabulary and the implicit-feedback
weight assigned to each type by the collaborative configuration. The weights
encode an ordering of intent strength that is a design choice, stated here
explicitly rather than presented as learned.

Two grains are maintained. \texttt{fact\_sales} is at atomic order-line
granularity and carries additive measures (quantity, unit price, gross revenue,
discount, net revenue) with a natural key of (order, product, order item) to
make loads idempotent. \texttt{fact\_interaction\_daily} is pre-aggregated at
daily product grain and carries additive counts (views, searches, carts,
wishlists, purchases) plus semi-additive distinct counts (unique users, unique
sessions), keyed on (time, product). Choosing a daily grain for telemetry is a
deliberate trade: it bounds fact-table growth and makes funnel slicing cheap, at
the cost of forbidding within-day session reconstruction from the warehouse ---
which is why the sequence learner reads the operational telemetry table instead.

\subsection{Business Rule Governance}

Recommendation-affecting parameters are data, not code. Weights, diversity
boost, rating thresholds, and discount-highlight limits are stored as system
configuration and every mutation writes an immutable audit row capturing both
the previous and the new value. This separates the tunable surface from the code
path and means a ranking change can always be attributed.

\section{Catalogue Quality Assurance and the Warehouse Load}
\label{sec:etl}

\subsection{Pipeline Stages}

The ETL stage executes: extraction from raw category feeds; parsing and
structural validation; category resolution; deduplication by product identifier;
a ten-rule quality gate; certification; and load into the star schema, with job
statistics written to \texttt{etl\_job\_runs} and dependency metadata to
\texttt{etl\_data\_lineage}.

Category resolution is a three-tier cascade. Sub-category assignment is attempted
first; on failure the parent category is used; and failing both, a
word-boundary keyword classifier is applied. The cascade resolved 228{,}473
records by sub-category, 215{,}556 by keyword, and 46{,}121 by parent category.

\subsection{The Quality Gate}

Table~\ref{tab:gate} reports the gate's outcome. Of 572{,}083 scanned records,
17{,}931 were certified and 4{,}653 were rejected. The asymmetry is informative:
the bulk of records were discarded upstream by structural validation (41 missing
names, 17{,}811 invalid prices, 43{,}584 unclassifiable), while the gate itself
removes records that are structurally well-formed but semantically wrong.

\subsection{Quality Dimensions}

Beyond the gate, the system computes a composite quality index over four
dimensions, each scored independently and persisted with the individual checks
that produced them: \emph{completeness} (fraction of mandatory fields
populated), \emph{consistency} (referential and domain conformance),
\emph{timeliness} (staleness of the most recent extraction), and an
\emph{overall} aggregate mapped to a four-level status. Persisting the checks
rather than only the score means a score regression can be localised to a
specific failing check without re-running the audit.

\section{Mining and Recommendation Models}
\label{sec:models}

\subsection{Collaborative Filtering}

The collaborative network follows NeuMF. Two embedding tables are maintained per
entity, one per branch. The GMF branch multiplies the user and item embeddings
element-wise, which is equivalent to a linear latent interaction. The MLP branch
concatenates its embeddings and passes them through layers of sizes
$[64, 32, 16, 8]$ with ReLU activations and dropout $0.2$. The two branch outputs
are concatenated and mapped to a single logit, passed through the logistic
function.

Embeddings are initialised from a zero-mean normal with standard deviation
$0.01$ and dense layers by Glorot uniform initialisation. Training uses Adam
($1\times10^{-3}$, weight decay $1\times10^{-6}$), binary cross-entropy, batch
size 256, and 20 epochs, with four negatives sampled per positive and
\textbf{fresh negatives resampled every epoch}. Resampling matters: with a fixed
negative set the model learns to suppress those specific items rather than to
discriminate in general.

Evaluation holds out the last interaction per user. Identity mappings between
database identifiers and dense indices are persisted alongside the checkpoint,
so a product retains its index across retrains --- without this, every retrain
would silently invalidate previously stored recommendations.

\subsection{Visual Feature Extraction}

The visual encoder uses a ResNet-18 backbone initialised from ImageNet weights,
with the final classification layer removed to retain the 512-dimensional pooled
feature, and all backbone parameters frozen. A trainable projection head maps
512 to 256 through a linear layer, ReLU, and layer normalisation. Inputs are
resized to $224\times224$ and normalised with the ImageNet channel statistics.

Freezing the backbone is a choice with consequences. It confines trainable
parameters to roughly 131K and eliminates the risk of catastrophic forgetting of
ImageNet features on a 17{,}931-image corpus, but it also caps the encoder at
features optimised for object classification rather than for product
discrimination. Section~\ref{sec:discussion} returns to this.

\subsection{Sequential Session Modelling}
\label{sec:gru}

Session trajectories are reconstructed by grouping the telemetry relation on
session identifier and ordering within group by timestamp. Sequences shorter than
two events are discarded. \textbf{Prefix augmentation} expands a trajectory
$[i_1, i_2, i_3, i_4]$ into its prefixes, so one long session yields several
supervised examples from the same traversal. Inputs are padded to a maximum
length of 10 with a padding index whose embedding is fixed at zero, and the
model consumes only unpadded positions via packed sequences.

The recurrent core is a single-layer GRU with 64-dimensional input embeddings and
a 64-dimensional hidden state. The final hidden state is projected back to the
embedding dimension by a bias-free linear map and layer-normalised, producing a
session vector. Candidate scores are then computed by weight tying:
\begin{equation}
\text{logits} = \mathbf{h}_{\mathrm{sess}} \, \mathbf{E}^{\top},
\label{eq:gru}
\end{equation}
where $\mathbf{E}$ is the item embedding table. Weight tying, Eq.~\eqref{eq:gru},
is what makes this a recommender rather than a classifier: it keeps the scoring
space identical to the
embedding space, so the model can score the whole catalogue in a single matrix
product instead of maintaining an output head per item. Sessions are sorted by
length and packed before the recurrent call, then unsorted afterwards, so padding
never contributes to the hidden state.

\subsection{Collaborative Denoising Autoencoder}
\label{sec:ae}

The autoencoder reconstructs a user's interaction vector from a corrupted input.
A 256-unit hidden layer with tanh activations maps the item axis to a 64-dimensional
bottleneck; the decoder mirrors this with a \textbf{bias-free} final linear layer.

Two details earn their place. First, the encoder output is summed with a learned
per-user embedding before decoding, which conditions the reconstruction on
identity rather than on the observed vector alone. Second, the decoder's final
bias is disabled. With a bias present, the model can lower its loss by learning a
global item offset, at which point predictions stop reflecting user preference
and merely reproduce popularity. Disabling it forces every prediction to arise
from the user latent vector. During training the input is corrupted with
probability $0.3$; the index reserved for unknown or guest users is explicitly
zeroed at initialisation so that an unidentified visitor maps to a neutral
preference rather than to an arbitrary learned vector.

\subsection{Context-Conditioned Attention Fusion}
\label{sec:fusion}

The fusion layer implements Equations~\eqref{eq:alpha}--\eqref{eq:score}. Its
dimensionality is asymmetric: the collaborative and denoised-latent signals are
64-dimensional, the visual signal is 256-dimensional, and the sequential signal
is 64-dimensional. Each is mapped to a shared 64-dimensional projection by a
per-modality block of linear, layer norm, ReLU, and dropout. The four projected
vectors are stacked into a tensor of shape $(B, 4, 64)$.

The query is the arithmetic mean of the collaborative, sequential, and
denoised-latent projections. The visual projection is deliberately excluded.
This is the design decision that distinguishes the layer from a symmetric
multi-head formulation: the three included modalities describe \emph{who the user
is and what they are doing now}, whereas appearance describes only what the item
looks like. Using the query to ask ``does this item's appearance agree with my
current state?'' would be close to meaningless. Excluding it makes the query a
genuine summary of user state, so the visual key is scored by its agreement with
that state.

Keys are produced from all four stacked vectors by a single bias-free linear map
into a 32-dimensional scoring space. Scaled dot-product energies are computed and
a salience term is added, the latter produced by a two-layer network with a
tanh hidden unit mapping each projected vector to a scalar. The sum is
normalised by a softmax over the four modalities, yielding a distribution that
sums to one.

The fused representation is the attention-weighted sum of the projected vectors,
and a prediction head of $64 \rightarrow 64 \rightarrow 32 \rightarrow 1$ with ReLU
activations and dropout produces the logit. Training uses Adam at $2\times10^{-3}$
with weight decay $1\times10^{-4}$, batch size 64, 35 epochs, binary
cross-entropy, two negatives per positive, and a 15\% validation split. Feature
tensors are precomputed once from the four frozen base models rather than
recomputed per batch, which reduced a 35-epoch run to 83.11 seconds on CPU.

\section{Classical Mining}
\label{sec:classical}

\subsection{Apriori Basket Mining}

Order baskets are extracted from the order-item relation, retaining only
transactions containing more than one distinct item. The algorithm generates
frequent 1-itemsets by counting distinct occurrences per item within a
transaction, then generates frequent 2-itemsets as all unordered pairs of
surviving singletons, evaluating each candidate by transaction containment.
Support, confidence, and lift are computed as

\begin{align}
\text{support}(A) &= \frac{|\{t : A \subseteq t\}|}{|T|}, \label{eq:support}\\[-1pt]
\text{confidence}(A \rightarrow B) &= \frac{\text{support}(A \cup B)}{\text{support}(A)}, \label{eq:conf}\\[-1pt]
\text{lift}(A \rightarrow B) &= \frac{\text{confidence}(A \rightarrow B)}{\text{support}(B)}. \label{eq:lift}
\end{align}

Pairs satisfying $\text{support} \geq 0.01$ as computed in
Eq.~\eqref{eq:support}, $\text{confidence} \geq 0.2$, and
$\text{lift} \geq 1.0$ are emitted in both directions and sorted by lift.

A rule survives only when both its confidence and its lift clear the configured
floors, so a pair that co-occurs no more often than chance is discarded even when
its raw support is nontrivial. The distinction between
Eq.~\eqref{eq:conf} and Eq.~\eqref{eq:lift} is what makes this work: confidence
is the conditional probability of the consequent given the antecedent, whereas
lift normalises that probability by the consequent's marginal frequency. A rule
can satisfy the former while failing the latter whenever the consequent is
independently popular, and such a rule is not evidence of a merchandising
opportunity.

Capping generation at 2-itemsets is a deliberate scope decision rather than a
correctness shortcut. Higher-order itemsets are combinatorially expensive, and
2-item rules are the ones a merchandiser can act on directly by adjusting
placement or bundling. Section~\ref{sec:discussion} notes the cost.

\subsection{RFM Segmentation}

Customer vectors are built from recency (days since last fulfilled order),
frequency (fulfilled order count), and monetary value (sum of fulfilled order
totals). Each axis is min--max scaled to $[0,1]$, with a degenerate range mapped
to $0.5$ to avoid division by zero. Clustering runs Lloyd's algorithm in
Euclidean distance for at most 100 iterations.

The implementation departs from standard practice in one respect worth
recording: centroids are initialised \emph{semantically} rather than randomly,
at the scaled positions corresponding to high-value, loyal, lapsed, and dormant
customers. This yields segment labels an operator recognises immediately and makes
the output stable across runs. It also introduces a bias, since it encodes the
segment taxonomy in the initialisation; Section~\ref{sec:discussion} treats this
as a limitation of interpretability-versus-determinism, not as an advantage.

\subsection{Interpretable Churn Scoring}

Churn prediction combines a continuous propensity with a rule layer, because a
probability alone does not tell an operator what to do.

Six features are extracted per customer: days inactive, cart abandonment ratio,
negative review count, mean inter-session interval, order count, and total spend.
Abandonment is computed as $(n_{\mathrm{cart}} - n_{\mathrm{purchase}}) /
n_{\mathrm{cart}}$ when a customer has added items, $1.0$ when an abandoned cart
exists and no order was completed, $0.5$ when an abandoned cart coexists with
orders, and $0$ otherwise.

Features are normalised against fixed ceilings --- 60 days of inactivity, 2
negative reviews, 30 days of interval, 5 orders, and 400 in spend --- and combined
as a linear score

\begin{equation}
\begin{split}
z &= -1.60 + 3.40\,\hat{d} + 2.10\,\hat{a} + 2.30\,\hat{n}
     + 1.30\,\hat{i} \\
  &\quad - 2.80\,\hat{o} - 1.20\,\hat{s},
\qquad
p = \sigma(z),
\end{split}
\label{eq:churn}
\end{equation}

where hats denote normalised features. Order count and spend carry negative
coefficients: they are protective rather than risky.

An eight-rule layer then assigns a tier with a named cause. Table~\ref{tab:rules2}
lists the rules. The final tier is the more conservative of the rule outcome and
the probability band, so a strong rule cannot be overridden by a weak score and a
high score cannot manufacture an unsupported claim.

\section{Implementation}
\label{sec:impl}

\subsection{Technology Stack}

The storefront is a single-page application built with React and Vite, styled
with utility-first CSS and a small design-token layer supporting light and dark
themes. The backend runs Node.js with Express on PostgreSQL 16. Authentication is
stateless JSON Web Tokens; the client presents the token as a bearer credential on
protected requests. Passwords are hashed with a per-user salt.

The mining tier is deliberately a separate, non-integrated process. It is a
Python service depending only on PyTorch, torchvision, NumPy, pandas, and a
read-only PostgreSQL driver. It has no HTTP layer and no dependency on the
application code. This separation is a design constraint rather than an
incidental arrangement: it means a mining change cannot break a checkout, and it
means the model layer can be reasoned about, versioned, and evaluated
independently of the web tier.

Identity mappings between database identifiers and dense embedding indices are
persisted as JSON alongside each checkpoint. This is what allows a retrain to
leave previously issued recommendations valid, and it is why the four learners
each store their own mapping file rather than deriving indices on load.

\subsection{Reproducibility}

Random seeds are fixed at 42 for the Python components and for negative
sampling. Hyperparameters live in per-module configuration files rather than
inline in training loops, so a run is characterised by a configuration plus a
seed plus a commit. Each trained component writes a metadata artifact alongside
its checkpoint recording training duration, epoch history, and validation
metrics; the fusion layer additionally records its mean attention distribution
per epoch.

\subsection{Data Preparation Detail}

Neural components read from the operational telemetry relation through a single
read-only accessor. Collaborative training builds dense index maps from observed
users and items, converts rows to weighted positive pairs, applies the
leave-one-out split, and samples negatives per epoch. Sequence training groups
rows by session, orders by timestamp, discards short sessions, and applies prefix
augmentation. Fusion training precomputes the four feature tensors once from the
frozen base models, which is what makes a 35-epoch, four-input model trainable in
under two minutes on CPU.

\section{Evaluation}
\label{sec:eval}

\subsection{Protocol}

All five models are evaluated under one protocol: for each held-out user, the
true next item is ranked against 99 sampled unseen items, and a hit is recorded
when the true item falls within the top $k$. The split holds out the last
interaction per user. The evaluation corpus comprises 50 users, 17{,}949 catalogue
items, and 3{,}938 interactions.

We report seven metrics. Hit ratio at $k$ measures whether the true item appears
at all; normalised discounted cumulative gain at $k$ additionally rewards placing
it early; precision and recall at $k$ measure list composition; mean reciprocal
rank summarises rank position. Because top-$k$ accuracy alone rewards degenerate
recommenders that surface a small set of popular items, we additionally report
\emph{catalogue coverage}, the fraction of catalogue items appearing in at least
one user's top-$k$ list, and a \emph{diversity index}, the mean pairwise
dissimilarity of recommended sets. Both are essential here: with a 17,949-item
catalogue and 50 evaluated users, an accuracy-only comparison cannot distinguish a
useful model from a popularity baseline.

Table~\ref{tab:beyond} is the more consequential result. The collaborative models
achieve competitive accuracy while surfacing only 9.6--16.3\% of the catalogue,
meaning that more than 83\% of items are never recommended to anyone in the
evaluated population. This is the concentration effect, and it is the failure
mode that accuracy metrics are least able to expose.

Against the strongest neural component in its own class, the fusion layer more
than doubles coverage relative to the denoising autoencoder (18.86\% against
9.63\%, a factor of 1.96) while exceeding it on every accuracy metric. Against
plain NCF it raises coverage by 22\% at equal or better accuracy. Against the
visual encoder, however, it is comprehensively outmatched, and we do not claim
otherwise: the visual encoder reaches 96.4\% coverage partly because it ignores
the user, which lets it draw on the whole catalogue rather than on a personalised
region of it.

Table~\ref{tab:attn} reports the attention distribution at the final epoch. The
ordering is interpretable: collaborative evidence dominates, which is what one
would expect on a behaviour-dense corpus; the denoised-latent signal follows,
consistent with its role generalising for sparse users; sequential intent receives
little weight, consistent with the sparse session corpus; and appearance receives
the least weight at 0.1238 despite being the strongest single model on accuracy.

That last point deserves emphasis rather than burial. The fusion layer assigns
appearance the lowest weight of the four modalities, and the reason is visible in
its accuracy figures: the visual encoder's strong performance reflects item--item
similarity, which is not the same quantity as affinity for a \emph{specific}
user. The attention mechanism has, in effect, learned to discount a modality that
is accurate for the wrong reason. This is an argument in favour of learned fusion
over fixed score averaging, and it is the single most interesting empirical
finding in this paper.

The distribution is stable across the final epochs, varying by under 0.04 per
modality, indicating convergence rather than oscillation. Training reached a best
validation loss of 0.0223 and a validation accuracy of 0.9949 over 11{,}814
training pairs in 83.11 seconds.

\subsection{Fusion Training Behaviour}

Validation loss over the recorded epochs remains in the range 0.026--0.044 with no
systematic downward trend after the early epochs, while training loss continues to
decrease. This is mild overfitting, and given 11{,}814 training pairs against
roughly 118K trainable head parameters it is unsurprising. The practical
consequence is that additional head capacity is not the productive direction;
more training pairs from richer telemetry are.

\section{Discussion and Limitations}
\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{A Result That Contradicts the Design Hypothesis}

The fusion layer was motivated by the expectation that combining four evidence
types would produce a model at least as accurate as the best of them. The
evaluation does not support that expectation with respect to the visual
modality. The fusion layer does not match the visual encoder on any accuracy
metric, and the reason is visible in the learned attention distribution: the
network assigns appearance the smallest weight of the four.

The honest reading is that visual similarity and per-user affinity are different
quantities, and that the fusion layer has correctly identified that they should
not be combined at equal weight. This is a more useful outcome than a fusion
layer that matched the visual encoder, because it explains why purely
content-based recommendation fails to personalise. It also identifies the
productive next step: the visual pathway needs to be conditioned on user
preference rather than merely concatenated with it.

\subsection{Principal Limitations}

\textbf{Corpus scale.} The behavioural corpus is 3{,}938 interactions across 50
evaluated users. This is small enough that ranking metrics carry wide confidence
intervals, and no statistical significance testing is reported. Conclusions are
directional.

\textbf{Signal asymmetry.} The event vocabulary is dominated by browse-intent
events. Views vastly outnumber purchases, so the collaborative component is
substantially trained on weak signals. The weight table in
Table~\ref{tab:telemetry} mitigates this but cannot eliminate it.

\textbf{Frozen visual backbone.} The ResNet-18 encoder is frozen at ImageNet
features. Its features are optimised for object classification, not product
discrimination, which plausibly explains part of why appearance contributes so
little to personalisation. Fine-tuning the backbone on the 17{,}931-image corpus
is the obvious experiment, and it is future work rather than a completed one.

\textbf{Sequential sparsity.} Prefix augmentation multiplies examples from long
sessions but cannot create sessions where none exist. The sequence component's
weight of 0.1297 should be read as a consequence of data volume rather than as a
judgement about the architecture.

\textbf{Churn rule parameters are not measurements.} The confidence and support
values in the rule layer are configured by the system author. They are excluded
from the tier assignment, which depends only on the rule conditions and the
probability bands, but they must not be reported as empirical frequencies. We
state this explicitly because the distinction is easy to lose.

\textbf{Semantic centroid initialisation.} RFM clustering is initialised at
hand-specified positions rather than at random points or by $k$-means++.
This makes segments stable and immediately recognisable, and it bakes the
intended taxonomy into the initialisation, so the segmentation cannot be
considered an unsupervised discovery.

\textbf{Catalogue imbalance.} Gaming and books are severely
under-represented in the certified catalogue relative to the source feeds, a
consequence of the resolution cascade acting asymmetrically. Recommendations in
those departments are correspondingly weaker.

\textbf{Second-order association rules.} Basket mining is capped at 2-itemsets.
Higher-order rules are not mined, so compound behavioural patterns spanning
three or more items are invisible.

\subsection{Threats to Validity}

The principal internal threat is evaluator bias: the person who designed the
models also wrote the evaluation harness, and the harness was built with one
model family in mind. The mitigation is that all five models are scored by the
same code path under one protocol, which removes per-model tuning of the
evaluation.

An external threat is corpus provenance. The catalogue is derived from public
marketplace data and the telemetry from a self-operated instance, so the
distribution of interaction behaviour reflects this system, not the wider retail
population. Absolute metrics should not be generalised to other domains.

A construct-validity threat concerns the visual model specifically. Because it
scores item--item similarity without a user reference, its high accuracy measures
a different capability than the collaborative models' accuracy. Comparing them in
a single table without this qualification would be misleading; the table caption
therefore records the differing evaluation population explicitly.

\subsection{Limitations of Scope}

The mining tier is a batch process with no online serving path. Recommendations
are precomputed rather than requested-time, which means the fusion layer's
per-pair attention, its principal advantage, is computed offline and cannot yet
respond to a live session. Closing this gap is the single most consequential piece
of future work.

\section{Conclusion and Future Work}
\label{sec:conclusion}

This paper described Cartify, a dual-layer commerce platform in which
recommendation is a consumer of an auditable analytical pipeline rather than an
isolated model. The pipeline certifies 17{,}931 products from 572{,}083 scanned
records through ten deterministic rules, records every rejection with a reason,
and loads survivors into a star schema accompanied by provenance and quality
audit relations. Above it, four representation learners produce embeddings of a
user--item pair from collaborative, visual, sequential, and denoised-latent
evidence, and a context-conditioned attention layer learns per-pair weights over
the four.

Three findings are worth carrying forward. The quality gate is not a
formality: a single category-resolution artefact accounted for 1{,}856 rejected
records, and none of it would have been detectable downstream. Fusion improved
ranking accuracy over its collaborative component by roughly a quarter and
recovered most of the gap to the strongest behavioural model. And the attention
mechanism assigned the lowest weight to the modality with the highest standalone
accuracy, correctly distinguishing item similarity from user affinity --- a
finding that would have been invisible under fixed score averaging.

The principal limitations are corpus scale, signal asymmetry toward
browse-intent events, a frozen visual backbone, and the absence of an online
serving path for the fusion layer.

Future work proceeds in four directions. Serving the fusion layer online is the
highest-value item, since per-pair attention is currently computed offline and
cannot respond to a live session. Fine-tuning the visual backbone on the
catalogue addresses the modality the attention mechanism has learned to
discount. Accumulating purchase-weighted telemetry to correct the browse-intent
skew in the collaborative component would raise the quality of the signal that
the model weights most heavily. Finally, a randomised offline policy evaluation
would let the coverage-versus-accuracy trade-off in Table~\ref{tab:beyond} be
resolved empirically rather than argued.
